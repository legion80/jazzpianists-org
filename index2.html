The Jazz Pianists

an introduction

Jazz is America's classical music.

Born in New Orleans, it emerged from African-American musical traditions reshaped by slavery and carried forward through improvisation and collective expression.

The piano rose with the music.

Portable enough to be in social spaces and powerful enough to act as an entire ensemble, it became the instrument jazz organized around.

The piano was jazz's laboratory.

At the keyboard, new ideas about rhythm, harmony, and form were tested—then released into the music.

Meet the jazz pianists.

Ragtime

Ragtime emerged at the turn of the twentieth century as a piano-centered, fully notated music built on syncopation and formal clarity. Its characteristic left-hand patterns create a steady metric framework against which the right hand displaces accents and melodic emphasis. Though largely composed rather than improvised, ragtime introduced rhythmic tension as a primary expressive device, laying essential groundwork for jazz piano.

Scott Joplin

New Orleans-Style

Early jazz piano grew out of ragtime as musicians began treating written material as a point of departure rather than a fixed text. In New Orleans–style playing, form, rhythm, and embellishment coexist fluidly, and improvisation becomes an integral part of performance practice. The pianist functions both as a rhythmic foundation and a flexible participant in collective musical texture.

Stride Piano

Stride piano expanded ragtime’s left-hand vocabulary into a more dynamic and unpredictable structure, alternating wide-ranging bass notes with mid-register chords. This approach demands both technical control and rhythmic elasticity, allowing the pianist to generate momentum independently of an ensemble. Stride represents a significant increase in pianistic range, harmonic color, and individual expression.

Fats Waller

Swing

During the swing era, jazz piano adapted to changing ensemble roles while continuing to develop as a solo instrument. Pianists balanced rhythmic propulsion with harmonic ornamentation, often implying larger textures through voicing and timing rather than density. In the hands of figures like Art Tatum, swing-era piano became a vehicle for extreme harmonic reinterpretation and virtuosic illusion.

Teddy Wilson

Bebop

Bebop marked a decisive shift in jazz language, prioritizing rapid tempos, asymmetrical phrasing, and advanced harmonic movement. Piano playing became more linear, with the right hand articulating extended melodic lines while the left hand supplied sparse, rhythmically displaced support. This period represents one of the clearest stylistic ruptures in the history of jazz piano.

Bud Powell

Cool Jazz / Third Stream

Cool jazz favored clarity, restraint, and formal balance, often emphasizing long melodic lines and controlled rhythmic flow. Pianists explored linear counterpoint and harmonic transparency, reducing overt rhythmic emphasis in favor of continuity and precision. The result is a style that foregrounds structure and proportion over expressive immediacy.

Bill Evans

Mainstream Jazz

As earlier innovations stabilized, mainstream jazz piano consolidated elements of swing, bebop, and blues into a highly refined performance practice. Technical fluency, tonal clarity, and stylistic consistency became central values. Rather than redefining the language, this approach deepened and polished it, affirming continuity within the tradition.

Oscar Peterson

Hard Bop

Hard bop reintroduced emphatic rhythm, blues-inflected harmony, and gospel-derived gestures into post-bebop jazz. Pianists favored strong thematic material, driving accompaniment patterns, and clear formal outlines. The style balances harmonic sophistication with directness, reinforcing the physical and rhythmic dimensions of jazz piano.

Horace Silver

Avant-Garde

The avant-garde challenged established assumptions about harmony, meter, form, and instrumental function. Pianists explored dense textures, irregular rhythmic groupings, and extended techniques, often blurring distinctions between composition and improvisation. This movement expanded the expressive and structural possibilities of the piano within jazz.

Cecil Taylor

Fusion

Fusion emerged as jazz incorporated electric instruments, amplified sound, and rhythmic influences from rock and funk. Pianists embraced new timbres and technologies, shifting the instrument’s role within ensemble textures. Groove and sonic color became central organizing elements, reshaping both form and interaction.

Chick Corea

Contemporary

Contemporary jazz piano encompasses a wide range of approaches, drawing freely from earlier jazz styles as well as classical, popular, and global traditions. Pianists often engage with extended forms, contrapuntal thinking, and personal reinterpretation of the canon. Rather than a unified style, contemporary jazz reflects an ongoing dialogue with its history.

Brad Mehldau
