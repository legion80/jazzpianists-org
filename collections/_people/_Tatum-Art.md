{%- capture bio -%}
Art Tatum was born in 1909 in Toledo, Ohio, and was nearly blind from infancy, a condition that profoundly shaped his musical life. Showing extraordinary aptitude at an early age, he received formal classical training but was quickly drawn to popular music and jazz. By his teens, his technical command of the piano was already legendary among local musicians, and he absorbed stride piano traditions through recordings of Fats Waller and James P. Johnson, internalizing them at a level few could imagine.

Tatum moved to New York City in the early 1930s, where his reputation exploded almost immediately. His performances—often in informal settings like after-hours clubs and cutting contests—stunned audiences and fellow musicians alike. Even established stars reportedly avoided playing after him. Though he worked with small ensembles and recorded prolifically, Tatum was most often heard as a soloist, where his harmonic imagination and pianistic control were unrestricted.

Despite his immense influence, Tatum lived a relatively private life and remained somewhat outside the stylistic movements that followed him. He died in 1956 at the age of 47, leaving behind a body of work that continues to astonish for its technical brilliance, harmonic sophistication, and seemingly superhuman ease. His legacy is less about repertoire or style than about redefining what was possible at the piano.

{%- endcapture -%}

{%- capture contrib_jazz -%}
Art Tatum fundamentally expanded the harmonic language of jazz. His use of advanced substitutions, chromatic inner voices, and rapid modulations anticipated the vocabulary of bebop by more than a decade. Musicians such as Charlie Parker, Dizzy Gillespie, and Bud Powell openly acknowledged Tatum’s influence, not only for his harmonic ideas but for his fearless approach to complexity and speed.

Equally important was Tatum’s elevation of solo jazz performance. He demonstrated that a single pianist could imply an entire orchestra—melody, harmony, counterpoint, and rhythm simultaneously—without sacrificing swing or spontaneity. In doing so, he helped establish the solo piano as a serious, self-sufficient jazz format rather than a novelty or accompaniment role.
{%- endcapture -%}

{%- capture contrib_piano -%}
Art Tatum permanently reset the technical and expressive ceiling of jazz piano. His seamless integration of stride, classical virtuosity, reharmonization, and rhythmic elasticity created a pianistic language that has never been surpassed in sheer command. While few pianists have attempted to imitate him directly, nearly all modern jazz pianists operate in a world he made possible—one where harmonic freedom, technical mastery, and improvisational ambition are assumed rather than exceptional.
