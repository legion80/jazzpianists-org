{%- capture bio -%}
Lil Hardin was an American jazz pianist, composer, arranger, bandleader, and singer whose career spanned the formative decades of jazz. Born Lillian Hardin in Memphis, Tennessee, she received formal classical piano training while also absorbing the blues, spirituals, and popular music that shaped early jazz. After brief study at Fisk University, she moved to Chicago in 1918, where she began working as a professional musician.

In Chicago, Hardin quickly entered the city’s vibrant jazz scene, joining King Oliver’s Creole Jazz Band. It was there that she met Louis Armstrong, whom she married in 1924. Their partnership was both personal and musical, and Hardin became a central figure in Armstrong’s early development as a solo artist.

{% include u/people-article-image.html person=page src="hardin-1971.jpg" alt="Lil Hardin in 1971" caption="Lil Hardin in 1971" %}

By the late 1920s and 1930s, Hardin had established an independent career as a composer, bandleader, and recording artist. She led her own ensembles, appeared regularly on radio broadcasts, and recorded as a vocalist and pianist. She remained active as a performer into the later decades of her life and died in 1971 while performing at a televised tribute concert in Chicago.

{%- capture contrib_jazz -%}
Lil Hardin was a key architect of early jazz, particularly during its transition from ensemble-based New Orleans style to a more solo-driven art form. She played a crucial role in encouraging Louis Armstrong to step out from supporting roles and pursue a career as a featured soloist—an artistic shift that reshaped the course of jazz history.

As a composer, Hardin wrote numerous works that entered the jazz and popular music repertory, including “Struttin’ with Some Barbecue,” “Doin’ the Suzie-Q,” and “Just for a Thrill.” Her songs were widely recorded by jazz and rhythm-and-blues artists. In addition, she was among the earliest prominent female bandleaders in jazz, navigating and challenging the gender barriers of the era.
