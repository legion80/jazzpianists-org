{%- capture bio -%}
Jelly Roll Morton was born Ferdinand Joseph LaMothe in New Orleans, probably in 1890, and grew up in the city’s uniquely rich musical environment, where ragtime, blues, French and Spanish dance music, and African American folk traditions overlapped. He began playing piano at a young age and quickly absorbed the syncopated styles popular in Storyville, the city’s red-light district, where he performed in brothels, saloons, and gambling houses. These early experiences shaped his lifelong belief that jazz was fundamentally a composed music rooted in rhythm, form, and orchestration—not merely spontaneous improvisation.

By his late teens, Morton left New Orleans and became a traveling musician, hustler, and entertainer, performing across the South and Midwest. During these years he refined his piano style and began writing down his music, claiming—controversially but not without merit—to have “invented jazz” in 1902. While this claim was exaggerated, Morton’s confidence reflected his genuine sense of authorship and control over the music he played. Unlike many of his contemporaries, he saw himself not just as a pianist but as a composer in the European sense, crafting pieces with clear structures, contrasting sections, and dramatic pacing.

Morton’s career reached its peak in the mid-1920s after he settled in Chicago and made a series of landmark recordings with his Red Hot Peppers for Victor. These recordings showcased his compositional imagination and ensemble leadership, placing him at the center of early jazz history. By the 1930s, however, changing tastes, economic hardship, and his own stubbornness left him marginalized. Late in life, his importance was rediscovered through the famous Library of Congress recordings, in which he narrated and performed his life’s work. He died in 1941, increasingly recognized as one of jazz’s foundational figures.

{%- capture contrib_jazz -%}
Morton was among the first musicians to treat jazz as a fully formed art with compositional integrity. His pieces—such as King Porter Stomp, Black Bottom Stomp, and Jelly Roll Blues—combined ragtime structure, blues inflection, and collective improvisation into unified forms. He introduced deliberate contrasts in texture, register, and ensemble color, shaping early jazz into something more architecturally sophisticated than the looser, parade-derived styles that preceded it.

Equally important was Morton’s role in documenting and theorizing early jazz. Through his recordings and his spoken explanations, he articulated concepts such as the “Spanish tinge,” emphasizing the importance of Afro-Latin rhythms in jazz’s development. At a time when much jazz history was transmitted orally, Morton’s insistence on naming, claiming, and explaining his ideas helped establish jazz as a music with lineage, authorship, and intellectual seriousness.
{%- endcapture -%}

{%- capture contrib_piano -%}
As a pianist, Jelly Roll Morton fused ragtime precision with blues expression and a relaxed rhythmic feel that anticipated swing. His left hand moved fluidly between stride-like patterns, habanera rhythms, and broken bass figures, while his right hand balanced ornamented melodies with sharp rhythmic attack. Unlike later virtuosic pianists, Morton’s playing prioritized clarity, form, and ensemble awareness, setting a template for jazz piano as both a rhythmic engine and a compositional voice rather than a purely improvisational display.
